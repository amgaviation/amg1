# 04 — Phone Playbook: Scripts, Voicemail, and Objections

AMG Aviation Group · North Lauderdale, FL · (954) 408-1730 · information@amgaviationgroup.com

Read this before the first call of the day. It is written to be said out loud.

---

## Ground rules that apply to every call on this page

- Manual dial only. No auto-dialer, no bulk SMS, no ringless voicemail.
- Florida is two-party consent for recording (Fla. Stat. §934.03). Do not record calls — a solo operator has no reliable way to capture consent on every call. Write notes instead.
- No major sales presentation over the phone. The pattern AMG operates under is: short call, then a face-to-face or a written agreement to finish the sale. That is the §501.604(3) exemption posture under the Florida Telemarketing Act — and it is still unconfirmed with FDACS. Confirm it before running any volume.
- Keep a written DNC policy and honor removal requests on the call, no argument, no "just one thing." 47 CFR 64.1200(d).
- Never say "dispatch." AMG coordinates.
- Never say "we verify the pilot." The correct phrase is in the objection section and it is not optional.
- Never quote per flight, per leg, per hour, or one all-in delivered price to move an airplane. The fee is for a service.
- Never promise availability, 24/7, nationwide, a number of pilots, or a mission count.

## 1. The shop cold call

Target: independent maintenance shops, avionics shops, paint and interior shops in Florida and the Southeast. The shop is a **referral source, never the customer.** The owner contracts with AMG and the owner pays the pilot directly.

### Opener (say this, then stop talking)

> "Hey — is this the shop's scheduler or the DOM? … Good. My name's Tony Gonzalez, I'm a corporate pilot out of North Lauderdale. I run a small outfit called AMG. I'm not selling you anything and I'm not looking for work on your ramp. I've got one question and then I'll get off your phone.
>
> **When your customer's airplane is ready and the owner can't come get it, who moves it?**"

Then be quiet. Let it be awkward. The answer is the whole call.

### What to listen for

| What you hear | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| "It sits." / "It sits until the owner figures it out." | Best answer you can get. That's a billable slot they can't turn. | Go straight to throughput. |
| "We've got a guy." | Fine. Ask if the guy is ever busy, ever out of currency, ever not approved on the policy. | Position as the backup, not the replacement. |
| "The owner deals with it." | The shop feels no pain yet. Ask how long airplanes typically sit waiting on the owner. | If it's days, there's pain. |
| "We fly it ourselves." | Careful. Ask who flies it and under what insurance. Do not coach them. | Low priority, note it, move on. |
| "How much?" this early | They've had the problem recently. | Answer straight, then close. |

### The throughput line

Do not pitch price. Pitch the ramp.

> "The reason I ask — an airplane you can't get onto your ramp is a slot you can't bill. Same on the back end. If it's done and it's still sitting on your line four days later, that's your square footage, not the owner's.
>
> What I do is coordinate a qualified contract pilot to move it. The owner hires me, the owner pays the pilot directly, and my invoice is one line — a coordination fee. I don't mark anything up, I don't take a cut of the pilot's day rate, and I don't take a referral fee off you. You just stop having airplanes parked on you."

If they ask whether you pay them for referrals:

> "If we ever set that up, it goes to the shop as a company — against your invoice, with a W-9. Never to a person. I'm not putting anybody's job at risk over a couple hundred bucks."

(That is Fla. Stat. §838.16 territory. Commercial bribery is a third-degree felony. Employer consent is the line. Do not improvise here.)

### The close — end EVERY shop call with this

Not "keep me in mind." Not "if you ever need us." Ask for the specific thing:

> "Last thing. **Do you have anything in the next two weeks where a customer needs the aircraft moved?**"

Branches:

- **"Yeah, actually —"** → Get the tail number, the airport it's at, where it's going, the owner's name, and the owner's phone. Then: *"I'll call the owner directly, because the owner has to be the one who hires me — not you. Can I use your name?"*
- **"Not right now."** → *"Fair enough. Anything in the shop right now that's going to need to move when it's done — even if it's three weeks out?"* Then: *"I'll check back in two weeks. Is this the best number, or is there a scheduler I should be calling?"*
- **"Send me something."** → See objection #9. Do not just email and hang up.

Log the call. Tail numbers you heard, dates, name of who you talked to. The second call is worth ten times the first one only if you remember what they said.

## 2. The insurance broker call

Completely different motion. Do not pitch. Do not close for work.

**The ask is: "add me to the list you give people." It is NOT "send them to me."**

### Why you are asking for less — understand this before you dial

A broker who refers one named vendor has put their own recommendation behind that vendor. If the pilot bends the airplane, the broker's E&O carrier is now in the conversation about why they steered the client to Tony. Brokers know this. If you ask a broker to send you business, the professional answer is no, and you've burned the call.

A **list** is different. A list is information, not a recommendation. Brokers hand out lists all day — shops, appraisers, title companies, training providers. Being on a list costs the broker nothing and exposes them to nothing. It is a much smaller ask, and it is the ask that actually gets a yes.

You are also solving a problem the broker already has: the client who calls and says "my open pilot warranty won't cover the guy I found, now what."

### Script

> "Morning — Tony Gonzalez, I'm a corporate pilot down in North Lauderdale. I run a small coordination outfit called AMG. This isn't a sales call, I've got a question about how you handle something.
>
> When one of your Part 91 owners needs a contract pilot — recurrent, medical, the owner's out of the country, the airplane's coming out of an annual — and the pilot they found doesn't clear the open pilot warranty, what do you tell them?
>
> … Right. So here's my ask, and it's a small one. **I'm not asking you to send anybody to me.** I know a broker who names one vendor owns that recommendation, and that's your E&O, not mine. I wouldn't ask you to do that.
>
> What I'd like is to be on the list you hand people when they ask. That's it. Just one more name on it."

Then the reason you're worth listing:

> "The reason I think I belong on it — I don't put a pilot on a job until you or the underwriter has put it in writing. I pull the certificates, the medical, the currency, the recurrent completion cert, the time in type, the 61.55(b) currency if it's a two-crew airplane, and the pilot's own non-owned COI. Then I put all of it in front of you and I wait for an email naming that specific pilot as within the open pilot warranty or added by endorsement. No email, no flight. On a turbine I assume the warranty doesn't cover it and I plan on a written endorsement, 24 to 72 hours.
>
> I'd rather be the guy who makes your file cleaner than the guy who makes your Friday worse."

### Also ask (this is worth as much as the listing)

> "Two things while I have you. One — on a ferry under a special flight permit, do you want that acknowledged in the same email, or do you handle that separately? Two — for a turbine, realistically how long does an endorsement take on your end? I'd rather quote my owners your number than my guess."

Write down the answer. That number goes into every owner call you make from then on.

### Close

> "That's all I wanted. I'll email you a one-pager so you've got my name and number in writing — nothing to sign. If a client ever asks who does this, I'd just like to be one of the names."

Do not follow up more than twice. Brokers remember pests.

## 3. The owner call (inbound — someone with a problem right now)

He's calling because an airplane is in the wrong place. He is not shopping. He wants to know if this gets solved.

### Open

> "AMG, this is Tony. … Okay, tell me what you've got."

Let him talk all the way through before you ask anything. Then take it in order.

### Capture (write it down, every time)

1. **Aircraft** — make, model, tail number, serial if he has it.
2. **Where it is now** and **where it needs to be.** Airport IDs.
3. **Airworthiness status** — is it out of annual, is there an open discrepancy, has anything been deferred? *"Is this airplane airworthy right now, or are we talking about a special flight permit?"*
4. **Who owns it** — the legal owner on the registration. Individual, LLC, trust? *"I need to contract with the owner, so I need the name that's on the registration."*
5. **Insurance** — carrier, broker name, broker's direct email and phone. *"Who's your broker? I'll need to talk to them before anybody launches."*
6. **Open pilot warranty** — does he know the minimums? He usually doesn't. That's fine, the broker will tell you.
7. **Crew requirement** — single pilot, or does the policy require two? If two: SIC needs 61.55(b) currency inside the preceding 12 calendar months. Not an SIC type rating — 61.55(a)(3) exempts domestic US ops.
8. **Timing** — when does he want it there, and what actually happens if it's two days later? Get the real deadline, not the comfortable one.
9. **Who else is involved** — shop, DOM, broker, family office, an assistant who schedules.
10. **How he wants to pay the pilot** — because he is paying the pilot directly, not through you.

### Set the insurance expectation EARLY — before you talk about money

Do this in the first three minutes. If you set it at the end, it sounds like an excuse. Set it up front and it's the product.

> "Let me tell you how this works before we go further, because there's one step that drives the whole schedule.
>
> I don't put a pilot in your airplane until your broker or underwriter has said in writing — email, naming that specific pilot — that he's inside your open pilot warranty or added by endorsement. No email, no flight. That's not me being careful for its own sake. That's the difference between a claim getting paid and a claim getting denied while your airplane is in pieces.
>
> On a piston, sometimes the open pilot warranty already covers a guy and that's quick. **On a turbine, I plan on the warranty not helping us and I plan on a written endorsement. That's typically 24 to 72 hours on the underwriter's clock, not mine.** So if you tell me tomorrow morning on a jet, I'm going to tell you what's realistic today instead of telling you yes and calling you back Thursday with bad news."

Language discipline in this section — say it exactly this way:

> "We confirm certificates, currency and time-in-type, and we put the pilot's qualifications in front of your broker for written approval before the flight."

Never "we verify the pilot." That sentence creates a duty of care nobody is insuring.

### Money — say all of it, plainly

> "Here's the structure, and it's the same every time.
>
> You contract with me. You pay the pilot **directly** — I never touch trip funds. My invoice has one line on it: the coordination fee. I don't mark up the pilot, I don't mark up fuel, I don't take anything from the shop, and I don't rebill you for anything except an out-of-pocket I fronted at cost, like a positioning airline ticket, with the receipt attached.
>
> Coordination fee is $495 on a piston, $895 on a turboprop or light jet, for the first two days. Beyond two days it scales — $150 a day piston, $250 a day turboprop and light jet — and on anything past a couple of days I cap the total so the fee stays inside about a quarter to a third of what you're paying the pilot. On a one-day job I won't pretend otherwise: the fee is a big share of a single pilot day, because the work is the same whether the leg is forty-five minutes or four hours.
>
> Pilot day rates are the pilot's, and they run roughly: piston ferry four to six hundred; turboprop or light jet ferry seven to twelve; turboprop trip twelve hundred to twenty-one; light jet trip fifteen hundred to twenty-five. Phenom 300, CJ3+, CJ4, PC-24 — those I quote individually."

### If it's a ferry out of annual

> "One more piece. If it's out of annual or there's an open discrepancy, that airplane moves on a special flight permit under 21.197. **You or your A&P/IA files the 8130-6 — I can't be the applicant and I won't sign it.** The FSDO or a DAR issues the 8130-7 with operating limitations attached to it, and I need a copy of both in the trip file before anybody launches. Those limitations are binding under 91.9(a), so the pilot needs to have actually read them.
>
> And there's no continuing authorization on this — 21.197(c) isn't available to a Part 91 owner. Every ferry is its own permit."

### What NOT to promise on this call

- Not a date, not a time, not a name — until the broker's email exists.
- Not "I've got a guy." You have candidates until one is approved.
- Not a price to move the airplane. You quote a coordination fee and you quote the range the pilot will charge.
- Not the pilot's suitability as your judgment. The broker approves. The PIC decides go/no-go, fuel, route, release. Say so:

> "One thing I want to be clear about — once the pilot's in the seat, the go/no-go is his. Weather, fuel, route, whether that airplane is airworthy for that flight, that's the PIC and it's you as owner. I don't get a vote and I don't want one."

### Close

> "Give me until [specific time] and I'll come back to you with who I've got and where we are with your broker. If your broker's slow, I'll tell you that too — I'm not going to sit on it."

## 5. Objection handling

Answer in his words. Short. Do not defend — explain and move.

---

### 1. "Why would I pay you when I can just call a pilot I know?"

The hardest one. Answer it honestly or lose all your credibility for the rest of the call.

> "Honestly? If you've got a guy, you know he's current, and your broker's already approved him — call him. I'm not going to pretend I add something there, and I'd rather tell you that than take $495 to introduce you to somebody you already have.
>
> Where I'm worth it is the other version of that call. You call your guy, he's on a trip. You call the next guy, he's out of currency. Now it's Tuesday, you're calling around, and you still don't know if whoever says yes clears your policy — because you haven't asked your broker, and the broker's going to take a day or two on a turbine anyway. That's the day you're actually buying from me. I do the calling, I pull the paperwork, and I get you the email from your broker in writing before anybody touches the airplane.
>
> Most owners use me as the backup, not the first call. That's the right way to use me."

---

### 2. "Who employs the pilot?"

Every W-2 employer-of-record competitor sells exactly the thing AMG refuses. Have the real answer.

> "Not me. The pilot's an independent contractor and he contracts with you. You pay him directly, he gives you a W-9, he carries his own non-owned aircraft liability, and he's named on your policy by your broker before he flies.
>
> There are outfits that'll W-2 the pilot and put him on their payroll. That's a real service and some owners want it. Understand what it changes though: when a company employs the crew and puts them in an airplane for a customer, that company starts looking a lot like it's furnishing transportation, and now you're in a conversation about operational control and Part 135 that you did not sign up for. I don't want that conversation and I don't want you in it.
>
> **AMG never furnishes the aircraft and never furnishes crew as a package with one.** You own the airplane, you hire the pilot, the PIC has the authority. I coordinate. That's a narrower business than my competitors' and I built it that way on purpose."

---

### 3. "What if the pilot bends my airplane?"

> "Then it's a hull claim on your policy, same as if you'd hired him yourself — which is exactly why I won't move without your broker naming him in writing first. Then it's a hull claim on your policy, same as if you'd hired him yourself — which is exactly why I won't move without your broker naming him in writing first. I can't tell you how a carrier will handle a claim; nobody outside that carrier can. What I can tell you is that 'the pilot was never approved' is one of the arguments they can make, and it's the one argument I can take off the table before the airplane moves.
>
> On top of that, the pilot carries his own non-owned aircraft liability and I get a copy of the COI before he launches.
>
> One thing I'll ask you for in the agreement: a waiver of subrogation in favor of AMG on your hull and liability. Not because I expect a problem — because if there is one, I don't want your carrier paying you and then coming after a coordination company over a flight it never controlled. It's a standard endorsement, your broker does them all day, and it's a condition of me taking the job."

### 4. "Can you just get me a plane?"

Verbatim. Do not soften it, do not add to it, do not take a fee.

> "AMG doesn't provide aircraft. I can refer you to a Part 135 operator and I take no fee for that referral."

Then stop. If he asks why not:

> "Because the second I take a fee for putting you on somebody's airplane, I'm an air charter broker under Part 295 and that's a different business with different rules. I'm not in it. The referral's free and it's clean."

Never keep a list of available aircraft. Never say "we can get you to Teterboro Thursday."

---

### 5. "$895 just to make some phone calls?"

> "It's not the phone calls, it's what has to be true before the airplane moves.
>
> I'm sourcing a pilot who's actually qualified in your make and model, pulling his certificate, medical, ratings, last recurrent completion cert, total time, multi, turbine, time in type and 90-day times, his W-9 and his non-owned COI — and if it's two-crew, his 61.55(b) currency inside the last twelve calendar months. Then I put all of that in front of your broker and I don't stop until I have an email naming him. If it's a ferry, I'm also making sure the 8130-7 and its limitations are in the file before he walks out to the airplane.
>
> The $895 is that file existing. If you want to run it yourself, you can — it's a morning of your time and one phone call to an underwriter you may not have a relationship with you may not have a relationship with. If your time's worth less than that, do it yourself and I mean that."

If he's on a one-day piston ferry and the fee really is 100% of a pilot day — know that's a real weakness in the current pricing. Do not pretend it isn't. Say:

> "On a one-day piston, I'll be straight with you — my fee's a big share of the total. If it's a simple hop and you've got a name in mind already, this may not be worth it to you. Where it pays is a jet, a permit, or a week of coverage."

### 6. "How many pilots do you have?"

He cannot claim a number. Answer without one.

> "I keep a bench, and I'm not going to quote you a number off it — a roster count tells you nothing about whether anybody on it is current in your airplane and clears your policy. Some of them would be right for a King Air and none of them would be right for your Baron.
>
> What I do is source to the airplane. You tell me it's a King Air 350 out of Fort Lauderdale on Thursday, and I go to the people on my bench who are current in a 350 and will clear your open pilot warranty — and if nobody on it does, I go outside it and tell you that's what I'm doing. If I can't find one that clears, you hear that the same day instead of getting strung along.
>
> What I do is source to the airplane. You tell me it's a King Air 350 out of Fort Lauderdale on Thursday, and I go find people current in a 350 who'll clear your open pilot warranty. If I can't find one that clears, I'll tell you that same day instead of stringing you along."

### 7. "Have you done this before?"

New company. Answer honestly and still win.

> "AMG is new. I'm not. I fly corporate for a living — I've been the guy on the other end of this call, the one somebody's trying to get approved on short notice, and I've watched it get done badly enough times that I decided to build the version that isn't.
>
> What I won't do is invent numbers for you. I'm not going to tell you I've moved four hundred airplanes. What I'll tell you is exactly what happens on your trip, step by step, before you commit to anything — and every one of those steps is verifiable. Your broker's email either exists or it doesn't. The 8130-7 is either in the file or it isn't. You don't have to trust my history to check my work."

---

### 8. "Can you do it cheaper?"

> "The pilot's rate isn't mine to discount — you're paying him directly and I don't take a piece of it, so there's nothing for me to shave there.
>
> My fee I'll scope, not discount. If it's a straight repositioning on an airworthy airplane and your broker already covers the pilot under the open pilot warranty, there's less work in it and the number reflects that. If it's out of annual with a permit and an endorsement, there's more, and it's going to cost more. Tell me which one you've got and I'll price the actual job.
>
> My fee I'll scope, not discount. Days one and two are the base by aircraft class. If it's a straight repositioning on an airworthy airplane and your broker already covers the pilot under the open pilot warranty, there's less work in it and the number reflects that. If it's out of annual and moving under a special flight permit, there's a documented permit-ferry supplement of $250, because the permit and its operating limitations are real extra work. What I won't do is cut the fee and cut the file. That's the part you're buying."

### 9. "Send me something and I'll look at it."

The brush-off. Don't fight it — convert it.

> "Happy to. Two things so I send you the right thing instead of a brochure.
>
> One — is it fair to say the airplanes that sit on you are mostly [pistons / turboprops / jets]? Two — **do you have anything in the next two weeks where a customer needs the aircraft moved?** Even a maybe.
>
> … Okay. I'll get that to you today. I'll follow up Thursday morning — is this the number, or should I go through your scheduler?"

Send it same day. One page. Follow up when you said you would. If two follow-ups get nothing, put them on a 90-day list and stop calling.

### 10. "We already have someone."

> "Good — I'd be worried about a shop that didn't. I'm not calling to replace him.
>
> Two questions. Is he ever unavailable when you need him? And when he is, what happens to the airplane?
>
> … That's the call I want. I'm the second number, not the first. Costs you nothing to have me in the phone, and the day your guy's on a trip and there's a Baron sitting on your line, you've got somebody to call who already knows how this works."

---

### 11. "I need it tomorrow morning." (on a jet)

Do not say yes. Do not say no. Give him the real clock and a real option.

> "Let me tell you what's real, and then you decide.
>
> On a jet I assume your open pilot warranty doesn't get us there, which means we need a written endorsement naming the pilot. That's the underwriter's clock, and it's usually 24 to 72 hours. I can push, I can have everything in your broker's inbox within a couple hours today so the file's complete when they open it, and sometimes it comes back same day. I can't promise tomorrow morning, and anybody who does is either not asking your underwriter or planning to launch without the approval. You don't want the second one — that's the flight where a claim doesn't get paid.
>
> Here's what I'd do. Give me your broker's direct line right now and let me get the package in today. Realistic target is [day+2] morning. And if tomorrow genuinely can't slip and it's people that need to move rather than the airplane, I'll give you a Part 135 operator's number and I don't take anything for that."

Sell the approval step as the product, not as a delay.

---

### 12. "Just bill me net 30."

> "On my fee it's half on signature and half when the pilot accepts. That part's fixed — it's in the agreement — but the second half only lands if there's a pilot to send.
>
> The pilot's a different animal. You're paying him directly, not through me, so his terms are between you and him, and most contract guys want a deposit or day-of payment. I can't put him on net 30 on your behalf because I don't handle the money and I'm not going to start. The day I'm collecting trip funds and paying them out, I'm a management company with somebody else's money in my account, and that's not the business.
>
> So: pilot gets paid on his terms, direct. My one-line fee I'll invoice you and we can discuss terms on it."

---

### 13. "Can I just pay you and you sort it all out?"

> "No, and I want to be clear about why, because it sounds like I'm making it harder than it needs to be.
>
> The moment I'm collecting money for a flight and paying out the pilot and the expenses, I look like I'm selling transportation. That's the line between what I do and illegal charter, and it isn't a technicality — that's the thing that gets an operation shut down and a claim denied. So: you pay the pilot, I invoice one line for coordination, and nothing else goes through me. The only exception is if I front an airline ticket to position the pilot, and that comes back to me at cost with the receipt attached — never as an hourly or a per-leg number."

---

### 14. "The shop said they'd handle it — can I just have them pay you?"

> "No. The owner contracts with me and the owner pays me, every time, even when the shop's the one who found me. If I take money from the shop, the shop is my customer and the shop doesn't own the airplane — that structure is a mess I won't get into.
>
> Put me in touch with the owner. The shop stays exactly what it is: the guy who told him to call me."

---

### 15. "Can your guy just fly with my son / my partner so the insurance is happy?"

Mentor work. This is where the private-pilot problem breaks most often.

> "I can coordinate a mentor pilot, yes. One hard line: he'll hold a commercial or ATP. Never a private certificate. A private pilot can't act as PIC for compensation — 61.113(a) — and mentor work is exactly where people forget that because it feels like instructing rather than flying.
>
> And it goes through the same gate as everything else. Your broker names him in writing, and if the policy needs two crew we confirm 61.55(b) currency inside the last twelve calendar months. Then he flies."

## 6. Qualifying questions — every first call

Ask all of them. In this order. Write down the answers.

1. Make, model, tail number. Where is it now, where does it need to be?
2. Is it airworthy right now, or are we talking about a special flight permit?
3. Who is the legal owner on the registration — you personally, an LLC, a trust?
4. Are you the person who can sign an agreement and pay the pilot?
5. Who's your insurance broker, and what's their direct email?
6. Do you know your open pilot warranty minimums? (Fine if not — the broker will.)
7. Does the policy require two pilots in this airplane?
8. When do you want it there — and what actually happens if it's two days later?
9. Has anybody already been approached about flying it? (Sometimes the answer is "my nephew," and that's a conversation.)
10. Who else touches this — shop, DOM, family office, assistant?
11. Is there anything open on the airplane a pilot should know about before he sees it?
12. How did you hear about me? (Track it. It's the only marketing data that exists.)

## Disqualifiers — decline politely and get off the phone

Say no early. A bad first trip costs more than ten calls that went nowhere.

| Signal | Why it's a no |
|---|---|
| He wants an aircraft, not a pilot. | AMG doesn't provide aircraft. Refer to a Part 135 operator, take no fee. |
| Passengers are paying him, or anyone but the owner is paying for the flight. | That's carriage for compensation. Not AMG's business at any price. |
| Someone who isn't the owner wants to be the contracting party or the payer. | The owner contracts and the owner pays. No exceptions. |
| He wants one all-in price to move the airplane. | Never quote a delivered price, per leg, per hour, or per flight. |
| He wants AMG to file the ferry permit or sign the 8130-6. | AMG is never the applicant or signer. Owner or the owner's A&P/IA. |
| The broker won't put approval in writing, or he doesn't want the broker involved. | No email, no flight. This one ends the call. |
| The only available pilot holds a private certificate. | 61.113(a). Nonstarter, including for mentor work. |
| He wants a dry lease with AMG-supplied crew as a condition. | Classic sham dry lease. Walk. |
| He wants AMG to pay fuel, handling, or hangar and rebill it. | AMG doesn't pay trip operating expenses. |
| He wants AMG to make the go/no-go, fuel, route, or release call. | Operational control. That's the PIC and the owner. |
| He won't provide a waiver of subrogation in favor of AMG. | Pre-launch condition, not a negotiation. |
| Registration, ownership, or airworthiness status doesn't add up. | Stop. Do not talk yourself into it. |

Decline language:

> "That's not something I do — and I'd rather tell you now than three days from now. What you're describing needs [a Part 135 operator / your A&P to file the permit / your broker's sign-off]. If that changes and it becomes a pilot problem instead, call me back."

## Call log — capture on every call

Date · Company · Person and role · Direct number · Tail numbers mentioned · Airworthiness status · Owner name and contact · Broker name and contact · Next action · Date of next action · Result.

If it isn't written down it didn't happen. The second call is where this business is.
